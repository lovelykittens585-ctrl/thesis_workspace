 The beginnings of the World Wide Web started with Tim Berners-Lee (b.1955). He proposed an info management system which then turned into the web as we know it today. He created HTML, HTTP2, and the URL3.

It should be known that he didn’t create the internet, in fact people before, after and during his time were all those who were involved in creating the infrastructure for the Internet. In fact, this is what makes the history of the internet an embodied one rather than a linear, historical narrative.

I am specifically speaking to the many contributions of women of color, whose work goes largely unnoticed despite laying the groundwork for much of the tech industry.

Nakamura, L. (2026). The inattention economy: How women of color built the internet (p. 35). University of Minnesota Press.
"Women of color are the firsts and the lasts of digital technology, the first to be experimented upon as laborers and signs and symbols of modernity, progress, craft, and sexuality, and the last to be seen. Therefore they occupy a unique position in relation to technology’s uncertain future and its unacknowledged past, between possibility and technoskepticism."


The World Wide Web is a place, while the internet is the space that fills it up. Space is differentiated from place as space is oftentimes boundless. Place is usually allocated and definite, in this case The web is the internet’s access point, through webpages, servers and IP addresses. The internet is filled with connections, as it connects us locally, nationally and worldwide. I consider the internet a space, not just because it is a “network of networks” but because it is expansive, seemingly endless and generative.

Previously, I had mentioned HTML, the key word in the acronym is "hypertext". Hypertext was a term coined by Ted Nelson in 1963 and with this term he envisioned an ideal vision of the Internet and of hypertext itself. Before the Web, there was Xanadu, a hypertext project that he considered to be even more expansive than the web at the time.

He states,
"The computer world is not just technicality and razzle-dazzle. It is a continual war over software politics and paradigms. With ideas which are still radical, WE FIGHT ON. We hope for vindication, the last laugh, and recognition as an additional standard-- electronic documents with visible connections."


CosmicBook™, Nelson with Ian Heath (2003)-- Windows only.


His ideal view of the internet wasn't just one of interconnected networks and hyperlinks didn't just take you to another page, you'd be able to see visible connections.

There is a distinct push and pull between Berners and Nelson. The web that Nelson wanted isn't unlike that of Berners, the connections and networks we make are still there. What Nelson wanted was the internet to truly be a space to bridge connections beyond the hyperlink. (This page is a bad example of his vision)

It is true that the web enables us to connect with one another; Slowly, we become active participants. In the earlier days of the Internet you had to earn your place by understanding the network, the machine. Now, we have reached the point where you don’t necessarily have to understand the machine. It can be debated that the machine has been trained to understand us more than we understand it. Now, singular prompts can generate entire landscapes.
    We’re at a point in time where we’re seeing people pull away from machines, many people in my personal life utilize older tech, and have time limits on their social media apps. We’re seeing people wreck havoc online and offline because of the capabilities of LLM’s and generative AI. We want our privacy back, yet the internet keeps us coming back for more, whether it is new or old. What I want to know is whether or not pulling away from cyberspace is the right idea.

Something to note, as the web grows larger we work harder to understand and push the boundaries of the digital. From the beginning we’ve been giving the web our data, it then goes to categorize it, curate it to our tastes and suggest experiences. I’ve come to learn that the more we give the web, the more networks we create and the more metadata we need to define. We are images, text and media. The internet is a story about us and the more data we give up (consensual or otherwise) the more we become a part of the web. We’ve become inseparable from the web. The lives people have begun to live didn’t start with the introduction to the digital web but to that of the machine. As I'm writing this, I've been trying to figure out what the best way to write and present this piece is. You may be thinking that in order to have written this I need to be absolutely engrossed in the internet. I haven’t been engrossed in it, but I’ve been an active ghost. Through pulling away from an algorithm that wasn’t satisfying me, I’ve gathered my own images, sources and text to synthesize later on in the writing.
