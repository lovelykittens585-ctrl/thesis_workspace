“Our neuroses, emotions and attention are ordered by our computers. As if in a trance, we follow the collective pattern of feeling transmitted to us—collective hypnosis, a feeling of shared outrage, fear, anger, joy, catharsis, justice, revenge, pleasure. Online, all impersonal worldly events are experienced as intensely personal, even if we don’t play a role in them.”1
Konior, B. (2026).
The Dark Forest Theory of The Internet.
Polity.1

As stated previously, the internet was once a place that existed separately from us. Now, we can access it almost anywhere. Konior, touches on how the internet offers this illusion of self, control and agency. “Everything feels personal”. I do believe that we are offered this illusion, we play in it, we wade in it, we live in it. There was a point in my life where the internet felt so much more personal and so much heavier. Outside of whatever the current politics were, what I’m really talking about is this idea that my online identity shaped so much of my personal life(AFK) that I didn’t have an identity outside that space.

     It started with the recently released Kindle Fire (2011-2014).

It was a device that was an expansion of their previous Kindle e-readers. This was a real tablet, it wasn’t an iPad but it was all I needed. I soon discovered YouTube, Wattpad and social networking sites like Tumblr. Interfacing with the tablet was an important aspect to my fascination with technology, previously I had only really used time in the computer lab or my small Acer laptop.
   This tablet became a stepping stone for me, I soon discovered a love for content-creators, fanfiction and started developing my own music  tastes through YouTube. I became so engrossed in these spaces that I became a user myself. I started a Tumblr blog, I began to participate  in fandom and find communities I previously hadn’t encountered IRL.
This IRL self vs the digital self is something that’s stuck with me since I was in middle-school. People who knew me online were so separate from the person I was IRL . Online I obscured my age, race and explored my gender. IRL I was just what everyone knew me as: 5’3, Black, “female”.
Partial 3D-scan of myself. I'm still the same height. Also still Black.


 It occurred to me, very early on that the internet and myself were already embroiled in one another. At my current age, I feel like I know so little about what happens on the internet. I’m no longer in any fandoms, I’m not keeping up with content-creators and I’m barely posting my art. Despite this, my exploration of the internet as an alternative way of living our lives is out of a desire to unpack my own relationship with it.
 I was highly involved in cyberspace. I was on tumblr faking my age, making blog posts and moodboards about Japanese fashion. I was experimenting with my gender and sexuality, and discovering what fanfiction was. It was only when I started to get so embroiled in this space that I began to feel a sort of unease. This endless space was becoming something different than what I had initially grown up with, I stopped commenting on posts, got rid of all the identifiers in my bio and started deleting my history more vigorously. I became increasingly concerned with my privacy, I became afraid of saying something disagreeable out of fear that I’d be harassed. I went ghost and ended up taking a 3 year long break from any form of social media.

What I learned in those 3 years was that the internet was not just a space to fool-around, but one where you learn to become a netizen. It’s not enough to be a part of a community, you learn to practice politics and you eventually discover the seedy underbelly of some of your favorite spaces on the internet. During that time in my life, the lines began to blur between what was real and fake. I felt so disconnected from the space I was so active and accustomed to, yet began to realize the Internet and reality as we know it are more connected than I thought. Why else would I mourn the life I lived in cyberspace?

The internet is far too large to disconnect it from our own reality (or reality as we know it through the five senses). The second we needed a better way to connect and interface with each other, the more the Internet became a thing of its own. It is not necessarily a space that is separate from our reality, so to define my digital/online self and my IRL self feels antiquated. Nathan Jurgenson, presents the use of the term “AFK”2 when presenting his idea of the “Digital Dualism”3 fallacy. The use of the term AFK indicates a continuous growth that is neither separate or unimportant from the digital self, rather it enforces the idea that what we do online affects our offline lives.

“…some have a bias to see the digital and the physical as separate; what I am calling digital dualism. Digital dualists believe that the digital world is “virtual” and the physical world “real.” This bias motivates many of the critiques of sites like Facebook and the rest of the social web and I fundamentally think this digital dualism is a fallacy.” Jurgenson, Nathan. 2011. “Digital Dualism versus Augmented Reality.” Cyborgology. February 24, 2011. 3

  Legacy Russell utilizes this idea for her own theory of Glitch Feminism; Russell uses the term “glitch” in this context, as a new mode of being. Refusal, remixing and remaking comprise glitch and it is not divorced from its typical definition:
“A minor malfunction, mishap, or technical problem; a snag.”
Glitch is a celebration of malfunction, failure and utilizes these functions as a new way of traversing AFK and our offline life. This mode of being is important to me, and the concurring vision of the internet we’ll try and craft after everything crashes.

However, I’ve always been frustrated by the conversations around the internet.

  There are many conversations and theories as to why the internet is the way that is.I think of the notion of a “dead-internet”, a place where no human activity occurs in cyberspace and bots occupy 99%4 of the space. It’s reminiscent of a fear humans have had ever since automation became a concept; This idea that humans will be replaced with automatons. These fears have only gotten stronger due to LLMs and generative AI and it’s a fear I completely understand.

However, I’d like to point out that everyone my age was dropped off on the internet wherever it was.

But more than that, the theory feels true: Most weeks, Twitter is taken over by an argument about how best to practice personal hygiene, or which cities have the worst food and air quality, which somehow devolves into allegations of classism and accusations of murder, which for whatever reason is actually not as offensive as classism anymore. A celebrity is sorry. A music video has broken the internet. A meme has gotten popular and then boring." Tiffany, K. (2021, August 31). Maybe you missed it, but the internet ‘died’ five years ago. The Atlantic.4

  We did not get the chance, nor choice to define how and what we wanted of the internet until we were old enough to start becoming users. It becomes very easy to complain about the internet or use it as a scapegoat, yet we’ve barely worked out the semantics of the internet. There is a language to understand when using the internet, whether it be code itself, slang or etiquette. It progresses quickly, blurs and flattens culture, societal norms and symbols.

We followed people before us, the content creators, artists and users that had been here pre Web 3.0. We were not dropped off in Berners or Nelson’s vision of the internet, but we’ve been here to witness the fallout of it. Many of the people my age on the internet are seeking better experiences than what we had before everything was uniform (or ad space). People are getting into making their own websites and spaces, alternative social media, anti-social media; We want more control, more freedom and the transparency that we were denied from the very beginning.
  I’ve seen people pull away from social media and the internet entirely; They go completely analog or utilize single-use technology that feels more tactile and meaningful than that of a MacBook or IPad.

The frustrations I have with the conversations about the internet is why I look to Baudrillard and Russell, as their ideas can be utilized to create and expand on the vision of the internet we’re so desperately trying to imagine.

  The internet distorts all signs and symbols we’re familiar with, this why it is intertwined with our reality. To imply that the internet is not real because it distorts what we are familiar with is to do it a disservice. Similar to television or social media, this distortion of the image is not indicative of that image being “fake” or “not real”. The parameters in which we operate on the internet are reliant on working within the unreal, distorted and absurd. The act of breaking our consensus reality is threatening; with the act of surfing the web a whole new set of signs and symbols are needed in order to interface with the space.

The fact that the internet distorts what’s real through flattening, remixing and remaking what we already know makes us wonder why we continue to operate within the consensus in the first place. Our consensus reality is something we all participate in, yet are deeply unsatisfied by. As we continue to fetishize the past or hone in on the future, we ignore what the future offers us. The internet offers us a subversion of this, where the past, present and future exist all at once.5

Baudrillard describes the “death” that occurs when the signs of the real become the reality,
“It is a question of substituting the signs of the real for the real, that is to say of an operation of deterring every real process via its operational double, a programmatic, metastable, perfectly descriptive machine that offers all the signs of the real and short circuits all its vicissitudes. Never again will the real have the chance to produce itself”6
Baudrillard 1981,

If the internet kills the real, that means it can be rebuilt. I go back to subversion in both its literal and political definition. To overthrow or ruin; “...an overthrow from the foundation.”. Subversion is where the online and offline become one, where the consensus reality is “dead’; The internet then creates a new reality.
  This isn’t solely on an individual basis, but a collective one. Images, media and audience are simultaneously flattened and given a new context. This isn’t to say that the internet is neutral in this flattening, how much a culture (or subculture) changes on the intranet is dependent on the circulation of pre-existing and ongoing information. It then starts this feedback loop of flattening, appropriation, information dissemination and ending with the eventual creation of a new community/scholarship to unpack this new information.

I look at the shift in the overall landscape of the internet. As the internet seems to have become smaller and smaller as we’ve occupied it, it feels as if there’s only a few dedicated spaces owned by one of the three major tech giants (Google, Microsoft, Meta). There’s an overall lack of real, occupiable space on the internet.

Occupiable space. It isn’t to say that people haven’t or can’t create a community on these platforms, but the lack of transparency, addition of features that users don’t want and lack of privacy on these platforms makes it hard for these communities and users to remain on these platforms.
